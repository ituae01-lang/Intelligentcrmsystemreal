CHAPTER ONE
INTRODUCTION
1.1  Background to the Study
Customer Relationship Management (CRM) is essential for businesses that want to build strong, lasting relationships with their customers instead of just making one-time sales. In the past, most small businesses in Nigeria handled customer information manually using exercise books, phone contacts, or WhatsApp messages. These methods were simple and personal but became difficult as the number of customers increased. Owners started forgetting details, missing follow-ups, and struggling to see patterns in sales or customer behavior (Chatterjee et al., 2021). For instance, a fashion seller in Ozoro might remember a customer’s preferred style today but forget it next month, or fail to remind a regular buyer about a new arrival, leading to lost sales and weaker loyalty over time.
Over time, CRM evolved from basic contact lists to digital systems that store and organize customer data more reliably. The introduction of cloud-based platforms made CRM more accessible, allowing businesses to access information from anywhere without expensive servers (Gartner, 2024). Today, intelligent CRM systems go further by using data analytics to find trends and artificial intelligence to make smart suggestions. For example, recommendation engines can predict what a customer might want next based on past purchases, similar to how online stores suggest related items (Huang & Rust, 2021; Wedel & Kannan, 2022). These advanced systems help owners send targeted messages, identify slow-selling items, and encourage repeat visits. features that were completely impossible with paper records or scattered phone notes.
In Nigeria, Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) form the backbone of the economy, but most still use traditional methods. Research shows that many SMEs face challenges adopting digital CRM due to high costs, unreliable internet, power issues, and lack of technical skills (Afolabi et al., 2023; Eze et al., 2025). This leads to low customer retention, missed sales opportunities, and inefficient operations. For example, a phone dealer in Ozoro might overstock slow-moving accessories or lose a loyal customer because he cannot track when they last bought or what they complained about. Intelligent features like analytics dashboards and AI recommendations could help SMEs personalize offers, spot at-risk customers early, and increase revenue without needing complex or costly tools.
This project builds on these ideas by creating a simple, affordable web-based intelligent CRM system tailored for Nigerian SMEs. It includes data analytics for sales trends and an AI recommendation engine using collaborative filtering (inspired by recent advances in AI-driven personalization by Ming-Hui Huang & Roland T. Rust, 2021). The system aims to address local realities in places like Ozoro, where businesses need practical digital solutions that do not depend on fast internet or high costs. By focusing on ease of use and real SME needs, it hopes to provide a tool that helps small owners compete better, retain customers longer, and grow sustainably in a challenging market.

1.2  Statement of the Problem 
The core issue facing many Nigerian SMEs is ineffective and outdated customer relationship management, which directly contributes to poor business performance in a highly competitive environment. Customers today expect more than just good products or services – they want to feel recognized, valued, and understood. When businesses cannot deliver this consistently, loyalty drops, negative word-of-mouth spreads, and revenue suffers.
In practice, the majority of SMEs handle customer data in ways that are prone to errors and inefficiency. Paper records get lost, torn, or damaged by rain or fire. Phone contacts are scattered across multiple devices or SIM cards. WhatsApp chats pile up and become hard to search. Excel sheets, when used, are often not updated regularly, contain mistakes, or are inaccessible when the computer is down. These methods make it nearly impossible to maintain a complete, up-to-date view of each customer.
As a result, several serious problems arise:
Forgotten follow-ups: A customer who made a big purchase might never receive a thank-you message or a request for feedback, missing the chance to build loyalty.
Generic treatment: Promotions, discounts, or birthday wishes are sent to everyone the same way, so no one feels specially considered.
Missed opportunities: Without analysis, the business cannot see patterns like “this customer always buys batteries with chargers” or “sales of certain items drop after school resumes.”
High churn: Customers quietly stop coming because their needs are not anticipated or met, and the owner only notices when it’s too late.
Wasted time: Owners and staff spend hours manually counting sales, listing top customers, or preparing reports for loans or taxes.
Competitive disadvantage: Businesses using more organized or digital methods appear more professional and reliable, attracting customers away.
Additionally, even when some SMEs try to use free or low-cost CRM software, these tools usually lack intelligent features. They act as digital address books without analytics or recommendations. Advanced foreign systems are too expensive, too complex, or not suited to local conditions (slow internet, power failures, limited computer skills among staff). This creates a situation where Nigerian SMEs are stuck in the past while global and even some local competitors move forward with smarter tools.
Without an accessible, intelligent CRM that combines easy data entry, meaningful analytics, and AI-driven personalization, many businesses continue to lose customers, miss sales opportunities, operate inefficiently, and struggle to grow in Nigeria’s challenging economic landscape.

1.4  Significance of the Study 

This project offers real, practical benefits to Nigerian small and medium enterprises, which are the engine of local economies in states like Delta, Lagos, Rivers, and others. By providing an intelligent yet simple CRM, it enables business owners to move beyond manual chaos and start using data to make smarter choices every day.
For a typical SME owner, the system means
Spending less time on paperwork and more time serving customers or planning growth.
Understanding exactly which products or services perform best, so they can stock or promote them more effectively.
Building stronger customer loyalty through personalized attention – a thank-you note, a tailored discount, or a timely reminder.
Spotting problems early, like declining purchases from regular customers, and taking action before they leave.
Generating quick, professional reports for internal review, bank loans, tax purposes, or investor presentations.
On the academic side, the work adds value to the Faculty of Computing at Southern Delta University by demonstrating how students can apply classroom knowledge in software engineering, database design, data analytics, and artificial intelligence to solve real Nigerian problems. It serves as a model project that future students can reference, extend, or adapt – for example, adding mobile access, voice input for low-literacy users, or integration with popular local apps.
More broadly, if tools like this become more common among SMEs, they can contribute to Nigeria’s digital transformation goals. More efficient businesses mean more jobs, better services for consumers, increased tax revenue, and stronger local economies. In a country where many young people are turning to entrepreneurship, having accessible tech tools can make starting and sustaining a business less risky and more rewarding.
Scope of the study
The project focuses on developing a web-based intelligent CRM system designed for small and medium retail or service businesses in Nigeria. It includes core features such as user registration with role-based access for owners and staff, customer profile management, and interaction logging to track sales, inquiries, complaints, and other customer touchpoints.
The system also provides a data analytics dashboard with visual charts to monitor sales trends, top-selling products, customer segments, and overall performance. In addition, it includes a simple AI recommendation engine that analyzes past purchases to suggest relevant products or follow-up actions, along with basic exportable reports for business review.

It is developed using Python Django for the backend and HTML, CSS, Bootstrap, and JavaScript for the frontend. Data is stored securely using PostgreSQL or SQLite, while Scikit-learn powers the recommendation engine and Chart.js generates analytics. The system emphasizes affordability, ease of use for non-technical users, and practical relevance to Nigerian SMEs.
Limitation of the study
The project has limitations due to time and resource constraints. The AI system uses basic collaborative filtering instead of advanced deep learning, and testing was done with synthetic datasets rather than real business data because of privacy and ethical concerns.
It is built only as a web application accessible through browsers, without a native mobile app, offline functionality, or integrations with services like payment gateways, SMS, or social media APIs to keep the scope manageable.
Security includes standard measures such as password hashing and input validation but does not cover enterprise-level protections. Performance was tested with moderate data sizes, so very large databases may require additional optimization.
1.6  Definition of Terms
i.      CRM (Customer Relationship Management): The strategies, processes, and technologies used by businesses to manage and analyze customer interactions throughout the customer lifecycle, with   the goal of improving satisfaction, loyalty, and profitability.
ii.      Data Analytics: The systematic computational analysis of data to uncover patterns, correlations, trends, and insights that can inform business decisions.
iii.    AI Recommendation Engine: An intelligent software component that applies machine learning algorithms to predict and suggest products, services, or actions that are likely to interest a specific customer based on their past behavior and patterns of similar customers.

LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1   Overview of the Study Area
The field of Customer Relationship Management (CRM) has grown tremendously over the last few decades as businesses realize that keeping existing customers is often cheaper and more profitable than constantly finding new ones. CRM is no longer just about storing phone numbers and addresses; it has become a strategic tool that helps companies understand customer behavior, predict future actions, and deliver personalized experiences at scale (Chatterjee et al., 2021).In the modern context, the study area for this project centers on intelligent CRM systems — those that combine traditional CRM functions with advanced technologies like data analytics and artificial intelligence (AI). Data analytics in CRM involves examining large sets of customer information to discover meaningful patterns, such as which products are frequently bought together, which customers are most loyal, which ones are at risk of leaving, or how sales vary by season, location, or time of day (Wedel & Kannan, 2022). AI takes this a step further by automating decisions: it can generate recommendations (“customers like this one usually buy accessories after phones”), forecast demand, score leads, or even draft personalized messages (Huang & Rust, 2021). These intelligent features turn raw customer data into actionable insights, helping businesses increase satisfaction and revenue without manual effort.Globally, intelligent CRM is now a major trend. Large companies use it to increase revenue through personalization — studies estimate that well-implemented recommendation systems can contribute 10–35% of total sales in e-commerce environments (Gartner, 2024). The Gartner (2024) Magic Quadrant report for CRM platforms confirms that predictive analytics, AI recommendations, and personalization are now standard in top tools, allowing enterprises to improve customer lifetime value significantly. However, these advanced systems are typically built for large organizations with strong budgets and reliable infrastructure, making them impractical for most small businesses.In contrast, in developing economies like Nigeria, CRM remains largely basic. Most small and medium enterprises (SMEs) which make up over 90% of businesses and employ the majority of the workforce still depend on manual record-keeping (exercise books, phone notes, WhatsApp threads) or very simple software (free contact apps or Excel sheets). These methods are prone to errors, loss of data, forgotten follow-ups, and lack any form of intelligence or insight (Afolabi et al., 2023). Afolabi et al. (2023) specifically studied digital transformation in Nigerian SMEs and found that unreliable power supply, high costs of foreign software, and low digital literacy prevent most small owners from adopting even basic CRM tools, let alone intelligent ones.The rise of digital tools in Nigeria (mobile money, online marketplaces like Jumia/Konga, social media selling) has created both pressure and opportunity. Customers now expect faster responses, personalized offers, and better service. An intelligent CRM could help SMEs meet these expectations affordably, but barriers remain strong: high cost of foreign software, unreliable electricity and internet, low digital literacy among owners/staff, data privacy concerns, and lack of systems designed specifically for local business realities (e.g., Naira-based pricing, simple interfaces, offline-capable features) (Eze et al., 2025). Eze et al. (2025) recently examined barriers to intelligent CRM in Nigerian small businesses after 2023 and concluded that complexity, cost, and poor infrastructure make advanced AI features almost impossible for most SMEs, calling for lightweight, localized solutions that work with limited resources.
This project therefore operates within the intersection of CRM evolution, data analytics, AI recommendations, and SME digital transformation in developing contexts. The literature consistently shows that intelligent features improve customer satisfaction, reduce churn, and increase sales  but most research and implementations focus on large enterprises or developed markets (Huang & Rust, 2021; Wedel & Kannan, 2022; Gartner, 2024). Very few studies propose practical, low-cost implementations tailored to resource-constrained small businesses in Nigeria (Afolabi et al., 2023; Eze et al., 2025). This leaves a clear need for localized, low-resource solutions like the one proposed here: a simple web-based intelligent CRM that combines easy data entry, meaningful analytics dashboards, and basic AI recommendations without depending on fast internet or high costs.
2.2  Theoretical Framework
This project is grounded in two main theories that guide both the user adoption aspect and the technical data/AI process.
The first is the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) by Davis (1989). TAM explains why people accept or reject new technology. It focuses on two key factors
 Perceived Usefulness  how much the user believes the system will improve their work (e.g., the CRM saving time on reports or suggesting products to sell more).
 Perceived Ease of Use  how simple the system is to learn and operate (very important for Nigerian SME owners who may not be tech experts).
These two factors influence attitude, intention to use, and actual usage. TAM has been widely applied to CRM adoption studies and is perfect here because the system must be useful and easy for small business staff in Ozoro to adopt it instead of sticking with notebooks or WhatsApp.
The second is the CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) model (Wirth & Hipp, 2000). This is a standard six-phase process for data mining and machine learning projects:
i.     Business Understanding  define goals (e.g., increase sales via recommendations).
ii.    Data Understanding  explore customer data.
iii.    Data Preparation  clean and format data.
iv.   Modeling apply algorithms (collaborative filtering here).
v.    Evaluation  check accuracy and usefulness.
vi.   Deployment  integrate into the CRM system.
CRISP-DM ensures the AI recommendation and analytics parts are built systematically, not just as experiments. It aligns with the project’s focus on turning customer data into practical insights.
Together, TAM guides the design to be user-friendly and beneficial, while CRISP-DM structures the intelligent features. These theories support creating an intelligent CRM that is both technically sound and practically adoptable by Nigerian SMEs.

2.3 Related Works

A substantial body of literature exists on CRM, with increasing focus on intelligent enhancements in recent years.
Internationally, commercial platforms like Salesforce Einstein represent the state-of-the-art. Einstein uses machine learning to provide predictive scoring (likelihood of purchase), next-best-action recommendations, opportunity insights, and automated personalization (Salesforce, 2023). Research papers evaluating Einstein-like systems report improved sales conversion rates (10–20% in some cases) and better customer retention through proactive engagement (Huang & Rust, 2021). These tools demonstrate how AI can turn CRM data into real-time, actionable intelligence for large enterprises, allowing sales teams to prioritize high-value leads and tailor interactions automatically. However, the high cost and complexity of Einstein make it impractical for small businesses, highlighting the need for simpler alternatives like the one proposed in this project.HubSpot CRM incorporates lighter AI features such as lead scoring, content personalization, and conversation intelligence (analyzing email/call sentiment) (HubSpot, 2024). Academic reviews praise these tools for democratizing AI in CRM, making advanced features more accessible than before, though they note high subscription costs limit accessibility for smaller businesses (Wedel & Kannan, 2022).         HubSpot’s approach shows that even lighter AI integration can deliver value, such as identifying hot leads or personalizing email content, which is relevant to the simpler recommendation features developed here.In academic literature, recommendation systems in CRM draw heavily from e-commerce research. Collaborative filtering remains the most cited technique: user-based (find similar customers) or item-based (find similar products) (Al-Emran et al., 2023). A landmark approach is item-to-item collaborative filtering, which powers many recommendation engines by matching items frequently bought together. Hybrid systems (combining collaborative filtering with content-based or knowledge-based methods) address cold-start problems (new customers/products with little data) and are frequently proposed for CRM personalization (Nguyen et al., 2022). These methods are especially useful for small datasets, making them suitable for Nigerian SMEs where transaction histories are often limited and incomplete.
Recent studies explore deeper AI integration: neural networks for sequence prediction (what a customer will buy next), natural language processing for sentiment analysis on complaints, and reinforcement learning for dynamic next-best offers (Alzoubi et al., 2024). One comprehensive review concluded that AI-enhanced CRM can increase customer lifetime value by 15–25% through better retention and upselling (Huang & Rust, 2021). Another study by Libai et al. (2020) examined how AI personalization affects customer engagement in service industries, finding that targeted recommendations reduce churn by providing timely, relevant offers. These advancements show the potential for intelligent features to transform customer relationships, but they often rely on large datasets and powerful computing, which are not realistic for most small businesses in developing markets.
In African and Nigerian contexts, research is sparser but insightful. Several theses and journal articles examine CRM adoption among SMEs, finding that while basic CRM improves record-keeping and follow-up, intelligent features are rare due to cost barriers (foreign tools priced in dollars), infrastructure issues (unstable power/internet), skill gaps (limited training on analytics/AI), and cultural factors (preference for personal over automated relationships) (Afolabi et al., 2023; Eze et al., 2025). Afolabi et al. (2023) specifically highlighted how unreliable electricity and expensive subscriptions prevent Nigerian SMEs from moving beyond manual methods, while Eze et al. (2025) noted that post-2023 economic pressures have made advanced AI even less feasible for small retail and service businesses.
Olayinka and Adebayo (2023) examined CRM digitalization in Nigerian retail SMEs, concluding that simple web-based tools with analytics can improve customer retention by up to 25% in resource-limited settings. Their local findings directly support the practical focus of this project on affordable, context-appropriate solutions for businesses in areas like Ozoro. Similarly, Chukwuemeka et al. (2022) explored predictive analytics for churn in Nigerian telecom SMEs, showing that even basic models can identify at-risk customers early, though they did not extend to retail/service contexts or recommendation features.
Adeyemi and Oladimeji (2024) investigated AI adoption barriers in Nigerian micro-enterprises, finding that fear of complexity and lack of training are major hurdles. They recommended starting with lightweight tools that integrate simple machine learning, which aligns perfectly with this project’s use of scikit-learn for collaborative filtering. Ibrahim et al. (2022) reviewed digital transformation in African SMEs, emphasizing that open-source platforms can bridge the gap between global CRM capabilities and local constraints like unreliable power and low bandwidth.
A study by Okeke and Nwosu (2024) focused on AI in Nigerian retail, showing that basic recommendation systems can increase average order value by suggesting complementary items, even with small transaction volumes. Their results validate the approach taken in this project to use similar techniques for personalization in a low-cost CRM. Another work by Musa et al. (2023) examined CRM in Nigerian service SMEs (salons, tailoring shops), concluding that simple dashboards and alerts can reduce no-shows and improve scheduling a direct parallel to the interaction logging and analytics features here.
These works collectively show significant global progress in intelligent CRM and recommendation systems (Huang & Rust, 2021; Wedel & Kannan, 2022; Gartner, 2024; Al-Emran et al., 2023; Alzoubi et al., 2024), alongside growing awareness of adoption barriers for SMEs in Nigeria and similar contexts (Chatterjee et al., 2021; Afolabi et al., 2023; Eze et al., 2025; Olayinka & Adebayo, 2023; Adeyemi & Oladimeji, 2024). The literature emphasizes that while AI and analytics can significantly improve retention and sales, implementation in resource-limited settings remains underexplored (Eze et al., 2025; Ibrahim et al., 2022; Okeke & Nwosu, 2024). Very few studies propose practical, low-cost implementations tailored to resource-constrained small businesses. This project addresses that gap by proposing a lightweight, web-based system that draws on global advancements (Huang & Rust, 2021; Wedel & Kannan, 2022) while solving the specific local barriers identified in Nigerian SME studies (Afolabi et al., 2023; Olayinka & Adebayo, 2023; Musa et al., 2023)
2.4  Gap Analysis and Justification for the Study

Despite significant global advancements in intelligent CRM systems, several critical gaps remain, especially in relation to this project. One major challenge is accessibility and cost, as most intelligent CRM platforms such as Salesforce Einstein, Microsoft Dynamics 365, and Oracle CX are enterprise-focused, expensive, and require complex setup and reliable high-speed internet. These factors make them largely unaffordable and impractical for many Nigerian SMEs, while even their free versions often lack full AI capabilities.
Contextual Relevance – Global literature and tools are designed for developed   
markets with stable infrastructure, large datasets, and tech-savvy users. Nigerian SMEsoperate 
in environments with frequent power failures, expensive data plans, low digital literacy, and
smaller,  noisier datasets. Few studies adapt intelligent CRM to these constraints.
Complexity of AI Features – Advanced recommendation models (deep learning, transformers) require significant computing power and expertise, making them impractical for small businesses. Simple, lightweight approaches (basic collaborative filtering) are underexplored in local contexts despite being effective for retail/service scenarios.
Integration of Analytics and Recommendations – Many studies treat analytics (trends, reports) and recommendations (personalized suggestions) separately. Few propose unified, user-friendly systems suitable for non-technical SME owners.
Limited Local Research – Nigerian/African literature on CRM focuses mostly on adoption barriers or basic implementations. There is almost no work on affordable intelligent CRM with both analytics and AI recommendations tailored to small retail/service businesses.
This project is justified because it directly addresses these gaps by developing a low-cost, web-based intelligent CRM using open-source technologies (Python Django, scikit-learn, PostgreSQL/SQLite). It prioritizes simplicity (TAM-friendly ease of use), practicality (CRISP-DM-guided analytics/AI), and local relevance (works on modest hardware, minimal internet dependency for core features). By combining basic collaborative filtering for recommendations with visual analytics charts, it offers SME owners actionable insights and personalization tools without requiring advanced skills or budgets. This contributes both practically (to Nigerian SMEs) and academically (to computing literature on localized AI applications).
2.5  Conceptual Framework
The conceptual framework explains how the proposed intelligent web-based CRM system operates and how its components work together to improve business performance in Nigerian SMEs. The system begins with inputs, which include customer data (such as names, contact details, preferences, purchase history, and complaints), product information, and business context like sales goals and customer categories. These inputs represent the real-life activities and records of small and medium retail or service businesses.
The inputs pass through several processing layers within the system. First, the Data Management Layer stores and organizes all information securely in a database. Next, the Analytics Layer processes the stored data to generate summaries, trends, and visual charts such as sales over time, top-selling products, and frequent customers. Then, the AI Recommendation Layer applies collaborative filtering techniques to analyze past purchase patterns and suggest relevant products or follow-up actions. All results are presented through the User Interface Layer, where business owners and staff can log interactions, view dashboards, and receive recommendations in a simple and user-friendly format.
The outputs of the system include personalized product recommendations, visual performance reports, and actionable insights that support better decision-making. These outputs help improve customer retention, increase average order value, and guide stocking or marketing strategies. The framework is cyclical because new customer interactions continuously feed back into the system, refining analytics and recommendations over time. Overall, the framework directly supports the project’s aim of developing an affordable, intelligent CRM system that bridges the gap between basic manual record-keeping practices in Nigerian SMEs and more advanced AI-driven business tools.

3.1  Choice of Methodology
The Agile methodology was selected as the primary development approach for this intelligent Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system. This decision was based on the project’s nature, the time constraints of a final-year undergraduate work, and the need to build a system that integrates web development, database management, data analytics, and artificial intelligence components.
Agile is an iterative and incremental method that emphasizes flexibility, continuous feedback, and delivering working software in short cycles. It allows for ongoing adjustments and improvements throughout the process, making it ideal for a project like this. The recommendation engine, for instance, required repeated experimentation with machine learning approaches  such as user-based versus item-based collaborative filtering  and parameter tuning based on early test results. Similarly, user interface elements like the dashboard layout, chart types, and navigation flow improved gradually as prototypes were built and reviewed by the supervisor.
Time limitations also played a key role. With a final-year project deadline, working in short cycles of 1–2 weeks enabled visible progress and allowed early feedback to be incorporated without major rework. The project was a solo effort, and Agile’s lightweight structure  including a product backlog, sprint planning, execution, review, and retrospective  proved easy to manage independently.
The specific Agile variant followed was a simplified Scrum-inspired process adapted for an individual student. It began with a product backlog containing all prioritized tasks and features, such as user authentication, customer profiles, interaction logging, analytics charts, and the recommendation engine. Every 1–2 weeks, a sprint was planned by selecting a small set of high-priority items to complete. Daily self-checks tracked progress and identified blockers, while sprint reviews involved running and testing the working parts, noting issues, and gathering supervisor input. Each sprint ended with a retrospective to reflect on what went well, what was difficult (such as debugging machine learning code), and how to improve the next cycle.
Agile offered several key advantages in this project. It provided flexibility to refine features based on testing or feedback without restarting phases. It enabled early detection of issues, such as database performance with sample data. It supported incremental delivery, where basic features like login and profiles were completed first, followed by more advanced ones like recommendations and charts. Finally, it aligned well with modern software development practices taught in the Faculty of Computing.
The main disadvantages were managed effectively. The risk of scope creep was controlled through strict prioritization and fixed sprint goals. The absence of daily team meetings was replaced with personal notes and weekly supervisor discussions. Documentation was kept up-to-date by maintaining a separate log and revising this chapter as work progressed.
In summary, Agile provided the ideal balance of structure and adaptability to successfully design and implement an intelligent CRM system within the available time and resources.

Ozoro is a semi-urban community located in the southern region of Nigeria and serves as an academic and commercial hub for surrounding towns and rural settlements.
Southern Delta University provided the primary academic environment for the design, development, and testing phases of the intelligent Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system. The institution offered access to computing resources, academic supervision, and a structured research environment necessary for the successful execution of the project.
In addition to the university setting, the study focused on SMEs within Ozoro, including retail and service-based businesses such as mobile phone accessory shops, tailoring services, hair salons, cybercafés, and small retail outlets located around Ozoro Main Market and adjoining commercial streets. These businesses represent typical Nigerian SMEs that rely heavily on manual customer record-keeping methods and have limited adoption of digital customer management tools.
The selection of Ozoro as the study location was based on its relevance to the research objectives. The community reflects a realistic operational environment characterized by intermittent power supply, moderate internet accessibility, and varying levels of technological literacy among business operators. These conditions provided a practical context for evaluating the usability and applicability of an intelligent CRM system tailored to resource-constrained environments.
Data collection activities, including questionnaire administration, were conducted within Ozoro among SME operators, staff members, and student participants who simulated business users. All system development and testing were performed using locally available computing infrastructure within the study location.
Therefore, Ozoro provided both the academic setting and the real-world business environment necessary for investigating the design and implementation of an intelligent CRM system suitable for Nigerian SMEs.
3.3  Method of Data Collection
Data for this study were obtained from both primary and secondary sources to support the design, development, and evaluation of the intelligent Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system. The data collection approach was structured to reflect real operational practices of Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) while ensuring ethical handling of information and suitability for prototype system development.
3.3.1  Primary Data Collection
Primary data were collected through the use of structured questionnaires and brief semi-structured interviews administered to selected participants within SDU, Delta State. The questionnaire consisted of twelve items combining closed-ended questions, Likert-scale ratings, and limited open-ended responses. The instrument was designed to obtain information on current customer management practices, operational challenges, and desired features in a CRM system.
The questionnaire was distributed through two channels: electronic forms shared via WhatsApp and printed hard copies administered directly to participants. A total of twenty-five (25) respondents participated in the study, comprising fifteen SME operators and staff from local retail and service businesses and ten final-year students familiar with small business operations who participated as simulated system users.
The collected responses indicated that a significant proportion of SMEs rely on manual record-keeping methods such as exercise books and messaging applications. Key challenges identified included difficulty tracking customer information, limited access to analytics tools, and lack of automated customer engagement mechanisms. Participants also emphasized the importance of system simplicity, affordability, and minimal technical complexity.
In addition, short informal interviews were conducted with eight SME operators to obtain further clarification on daily operational challenges. These discussions provided contextual understanding of real-world business processes and informed the functional design of the proposed CRM system.
3.3.2  Secondary Data Collection 
Due to privacy concerns and limited availability of structured business records, synthetic datasets were generated to simulate realistic customer and transaction behaviour typical of SMEs in Ozoro. 
The generated datasets included:
Customer profiles containing demographic and interaction information.
Transaction records representing purchase activities across multiple product categories.
Interaction logs capturing communication events such as inquiries, complaints, and follow-ups.
The synthetic data were designed to reflect observed patterns from questionnaire findings, including repeat purchase behaviour, seasonal variations in sales, and common transaction value ranges within local SMEs. The use of synthetic data ensured ethical compliance by avoiding the use of sensitive real customer information while providing sufficient volume and structure for system testing and analytics model development.
3.3.3  Data Preparation and Storage
All collected and generated data were organized into structured formats including CSV and JSON files before being integrated into the system database. The prepared datasets supported testing of the analytics dashboard, recommendation engine, and overall CRM functionalities during the development phase.
3.4  Sampling Techniques

Sampling in this study was deliberately non-probabilistic and purposive in nature, as the primary objective was to design, develop, and demonstrate a functional prototype of an intelligent Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system rather than to perform a large-scale statistical survey or produce findings that could be generalized to a broader population. The sampling approach was therefore directed toward selecting participants who could offer relevant, informed, and contextually appropriate insights into the real-world customer relationship management challenges encountered by small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in Nigeria, particularly within semi-urban settings such as Ozoro.
Purposive sampling was employed to intentionally identify and recruit respondents who possessed direct, practical experience in managing customer interactions on a daily basis. This included SME owners, managers, or frontline staff actively responsible for sales, follow-ups, complaint handling, or record-keeping in retail and service-oriented businesses. The selection criteria ensured that participants were drawn from the target user group — namely, small-scale operators in Ozoro and surrounding areas — whose operational realities (manual record-keeping, limited digital infrastructure, intermittent power supply, and preference for personal customer relationships) closely mirrored the context for which the system was being developed.
This approach was chosen because it aligned with the exploratory and developmental nature of the research. By focusing on information-rich cases, purposive sampling enabled the collection of targeted feedback that directly informed system requirements, feature prioritization, and usability considerations. While the sample size was modest and non-random, the method was appropriate given the project’s emphasis on prototype validation and practical applicability rather than statistical inference or population-level representativeness.

3.5  Analysis of Existing System

Prior to proposing the intelligent Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system, an examination of prevailing customer relationship practices among small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in Nigeria, particularly in semi-urban areas such as Ozoro, was conducted. This analysis identifies the strengths, limitations, and inherent deficiencies of current approaches, thereby establishing the rationale for a more effective solution.
Existing practices can be categorized into three broad types based on common usage among SME owners and staff.
The first category comprises entirely manual methods. In Ozoro Main Market and comparable settings, the majority of small businesses continue to rely on paper-based records. Customer names, contact details, purchase records, and notes are typically documented in exercise books, ledgers, or notebooks maintained at the point of sale. Follow-up activities depend largely on memory or manual searching through pages, while WhatsApp isbfrequently employed as a supplementary communication channel for order placement, product photos, and informal notes, though it lacks systematic organization.
Manual methods possess certain advantages. They incur negligible cost beyond the price of stationery, require no electricity or internet connectivity, and maintain a personal, human element that many customers  particularly older individuals  prefer over digital alternatives. This direct approach fosters familiarity and trust in face-to-face interactions.
Nevertheless, the limitations are substantial and pervasive. Physical records are highly vulnerable to loss, damage from environmental factors (rain, fire, wear), or misplacement. Retrieval of customer history is time-consuming and error-prone. No mechanism exists for identifying patterns, such as frequently purchased items or repeat customers. Follow-up activities are frequently overlooked due to the absence of reminders. As customer numbers exceed fifty to one hundred, the system becomes disorganized and unreliable. Manual sales reporting is labor-intensive, prone to inaccuracies, and precludes meaningful personalization, resulting in uniform messaging that diminishes perceived customer value.
The second category involves basic digital tools. A portion of SMEs have transitioned to digital alternatives such as mobile phone contact lists, simple Excel spreadsheets, or free versions of CRM applications (e.g., HubSpot CRM basic or Zoho CRM free tier). Contacts are stored with brief notes, while sales data may be recorded in spreadsheet columns including date, customer name, item, and amount.
These digital methods offer improvements over paper records. Information is searchable, less susceptible to physical loss, and can be backed up via cloud storage or device memory. Spreadsheets enable elementary calculations (e.g., monthly totals), and free CRM applications provide a more professional appearance for communication.
However, significant shortcomings persist. These tools primarily function as electronic repositories, lacking analytical capabilities, automated suggestions, trend visualization, or churn alerts. Spreadsheet maintenance remains manual and susceptible to errors or corruption. Free CRM platforms often require consistent internet access, which is unreliable in Ozoro during power outages or network disruptions. Learning curves deter many users, and the absence of intelligent features limits their ability to enhance sales or retention meaningfully.
The third category encompasses advanced foreign CRM systems. A limited number of expanding SMEs adopt paid platforms such as Salesforce, HubSpot premium plans, or Microsoft Dynamics. These systems deliver comprehensive functionality, including data analytics for trend identification, customer segmentation, churn prediction, and AI-generated recommendations for next-best actions.
Their strengths are evident: they provide precisely the intelligent capabilities this project seeks, such as automated product suggestions based on historical data, visual dashboards, instantaneous reporting, and predictive insights. Such features enable larger organizations to enhance personalization and revenue generation.
For Nigerian SMEs, however, these systems are largely inaccessible. Subscription costs, frequently denominated in foreign currency, exceed the financial capacity of most small operators. Dependence on stable high-speed internet and uninterrupted electricity poses ongoing challenges in Ozoro and similar locations. Implementation demands technical expertise or external support, incurring additional expense. The user interfaces are complex, with extensive menus and options that overwhelm non-technical users. The systems are designed for enterprise-scale operations with dedicated teams, rendering them mismatched for solo proprietors or small staff in market-based enterprises. Consequently, even when trialed, adoption is often discontinued in favor of familiar manual methods.
In conclusion, existing systems exhibit pervasive deficiencies across all categories: absence of intelligent capabilities, excessive reliance on manual processes, limited scalability with business growth, lack of personalization, inefficient reporting, and inability to anticipate or mitigate customer attrition. These shortcomings contribute to reduced customer satisfaction, lost revenue opportunities, elevated churn, and constrained growth precisely the challenges the proposed intelligent CRM system is developed to overcome.

3.6   Weaknesses of Existing Customer Management Practices
Most small and medium enterprises (SMEs) rely primarily on informal or manual approaches for managing customer information and business transactions rather than structured digital Customer Relationship Management (CRM) systems. Common practices include handwritten notebooks, mobile phone contact lists, messaging applications, and basic spreadsheet records. While these approaches provide basic data storage, they present several operational limitations that affect efficiency, decision-making, and customer relationship management. The major weaknesses are discussed below.
One significant limitation is the absence of automation and intelligent support. Traditional record-keeping methods only store information without providing analytical insights, predictive capabilities, or automated reminders. Business operators must rely on memory to follow up with customers or track purchasing behaviour. As a result, opportunities for personalized engagement, targeted promotions, and timely customer follow-up are frequently missed.
Another major weakness is the lack of structured data analysis. Manual systems do not easily generate reports or visual summaries of business performance. Identifying sales trends, high-value customers, or frequently purchased products often requires time-consuming manual calculations. This limitation makes it difficult for business owners to make informed decisions regarding inventory planning, marketing strategies, and pricing adjustments.
Scalability also presents a significant challenge. Informal record-keeping methods may be manageable when customer volumes are small, but as businesses expand, the volume of data becomes increasingly difficult to organize and retrieve efficiently. Customer histories may become fragmented across notebooks, phone records, or spreadsheets, increasing the likelihood of errors and missed follow-up opportunities.
In addition, manual processes consume considerable time and effort. Routine tasks such as preparing sales summaries, tracking repeat customers, or identifying pending customer interactions require repeated manual review of records. This reduces productivity and diverts attention from core business activities such as customer service and strategic planning.
Customer retention is also negatively affected by the absence of structured tracking mechanisms. Without automated alerts or historical insights, businesses may struggle to identify inactive customers or address recurring complaints effectively. The lack of personalization in communication can further reduce customer engagement and long-term loyalty.
Furthermore, reliability and data security remain major concerns. Paper-based records are vulnerable to physical damage or loss, while unstructured digital files may be accidentally deleted or corrupted. In many cases, there are no backup procedures or secure access controls, increasing the risk of permanent data loss and unauthorized information exposure.
Finally, existing practices provide limited support for modern digital business operations. Integration with online communication channels, automated messaging systems, and digital marketing tools is typically absent. As customer expectations shift toward faster and more personalized service, traditional methods become increasingly inadequate for supporting business growth and competitiveness.
In summary, the primary weaknesses of existing customer management practices include lack of automation, absence of analytical insights, poor scalability, excessive manual workload, weak customer retention mechanisms, data reliability risks, and limited support for modern digital operations. These limitations highlight the need for a structured, intelligent CRM solution capable of improving efficiency, enhancing customer engagement, and supporting data-driven decision-making within small and medium enterprises.

3.7 Proposing a New System
Following the analysis of the limitations inherent in existing customer relationship management practices among small and medium enterprises in Nigeria, particularly in semi-urban areas such as Ozoro, the development of an enhanced solution was deemed necessary. The proposed system is a web-based intelligent Customer Relationship Management (CRM) application specifically engineered to overcome the primary deficiencies identified, including the absence of intelligent decision support, lack of data-driven insights, heavy dependence on manual processes, inadequate mechanisms for customer retention, and challenges in scaling operations as customer volumes increase.
The system is implemented as a lightweight, cost-effective web application that is accessible through standard web browsers on both desktop computers and mobile devices. It requires no recurring subscription fees, continuous high-speed internet connectivity, or specialized technical expertise for installation or ongoing use. The fundamental design principle combines straightforward data input mechanisms with advanced analytical and recommendation capabilities, thereby enabling users to make informed decisions and sustain personalized customer interactions with minimal additional effort.
The system incorporates several core functional components. It provides secure authentication and role-based access control, allowing business owners (administrators) to manage user accounts and permissions while staff members perform operational tasks. Customer profile management is supported through intuitive interfaces that enable the creation, modification, retrieval, and searching of customer records, encompassing contact details, purchase history, and contextual notes. Interaction logging is facilitated by structured forms that systematically document every customer touchpoint including sales transactions, inquiries, complaints, telephone communications, messages, site visits, and follow-up activities with each entry timestamped and categorized for efficient reference and retrieval.
An analytics dashboard automatically generates visual representations, including charts depicting sales trends across defined periods, identification of high-performing products, peak activity periods, and customer segmentation (e.g., frequent versus occasional buyers), thereby eliminating the need for manual aggregation and enabling rapid extraction of business insights. The AI-driven recommendation engine applies basic collaborative filtering algorithms to historical purchase and interaction data, producing personalized product suggestions and follow-up prompts — for instance, recommending complementary accessories to recent phone purchasers or alerting users to potential churn risks. Basic reporting functionality supports the generation and export of summaries covering sales performance, customer activity levels, and recommendation outcomes, suitable for operational review, financial documentation, or external reporting purposes. All data is securely stored within a relational database, ensuring integrity, reliability, and protection against loss, with limited offline viewing capabilities for cached information in cases of connectivity interruption.
The implementation leverages open-source technologies to ensure affordability and accessibility. The backend is constructed using the Python Django framework for secure logic processing, application structure, and data handling. The frontend employs HTML, CSS, Bootstrap, and JavaScript to deliver a modern, responsive interface. Data persistence is managed through PostgreSQL or SQLite, while the machine learning recommendation component utilizes the scikit-learn library. The design prioritizes simplicity and alignment with local operational constraints, including compatibility with low-specification hardware commonly available in Ozoro, minimal bandwidth requirements with fast-loading operations even on slower connections, an intuitive interface featuring prominent controls and clear labelling, and the elimination of foreign currency payments or intricate deployment procedures the system can be installed locally or hosted economically online.
The proposed system directly mitigates the shortcomings of prevailing methods by incorporating intelligent recommendation and analytical capabilities, minimizing manual administrative workload through automation of charts and reports, enhancing customer retention via early detection of at-risk individuals and tailored engagement, supporting scalability as customer volumes increase, and ensuring secure, searchable data storage. Tailored specifically for small retail and service enterprises in Nigeria — where interpersonal relationships remain culturally significant — the system provides technological support to strengthen these relationships and facilitate sustainable business growth.

3.8 Analysis of the Proposed System

The proposed intelligent Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system is structured as a modular web application comprising four primary layers: presentation, application, data, and intelligence. This layered architecture ensures separation of concerns, facilitating maintainability, scalability, and ease of future enhancements while aligning with established software engineering principles for web-based enterprise systems.
The presentation layer serves as the user-facing interface, developed using HTML5, CSS3, Bootstrap 5, and JavaScript to deliver a responsive, intuitive experience across devices. It encompasses the login interface, main dashboard, customer profile views, interaction logging forms, analytics visualizations, and recommendation display panels. The design adheres to usability heuristics (Nielsen, 1994; updated in Nielsen Norman Group, 2023), emphasizing minimal cognitive load, clear navigation, large touch targets for mobile users, and visual feedback on actions, thereby supporting non-technical SME operators in semi-urban Nigerian contexts.
The application layer, implemented with the Python Django framework (version 4.x), manages core business logic, request routing, authentication (using Django’s built-in auth system with salted hashing), authorization (role-based permissions), form validation, and API endpoints (via Django REST Framework). It coordinates data flow between the presentation layer and backend services, enforces input sanitization to mitigate common web vulnerabilities (OWASP, 2021), and triggers intelligence-layer processing when required for real-time recommendations or analytics updates.
The data layer employs PostgreSQL (production) or SQLite (development/testing) as the relational database management system. It stores structured entities including users, customers, products, purchases, interactions, and recommendations, with appropriate indexing on frequently queried fields (e.g., customer_id, date) and foreign key constraints to maintain referential integrity. This design supports ACID compliance for transaction safety and efficient retrieval even with growing datasets typical of expanding SMEs.
The intelligence layer integrates two primary functions: basic analytics and the AI recommendation engine. Analytics processing leverages server-side computations (e.g., aggregations, averages, time-series trends) combined with Chart.js for client-side rendering of interactive visualizations (line charts for sales trends, bar charts for product performance, pie charts for customer segmentation). The recommendation engine applies collaborative filtering using the scikit-learn library (Pedregosa et al., 2011; updated implementations in scikit-learn 1.5+, 2024), specifically cosine similarity on user-item matrices derived from purchase and rating data. This lightweight approach is computationally efficient, performs adequately with sparse SME datasets, and avoids the resource demands of deep learning models (Huang & Rust, 2021).
In practical operation, a staff member authenticates via the presentation layer, triggering the application layer to load the dashboard. Upon logging a sale or viewing a customer profile, data is persisted through the data layer. The intelligence layer processes historical records to compute trends and recommendations, returning results to the application layer for rendering in the interface. This workflow minimizes latency, supports offline viewing of cached views where feasible, and aligns with usability requirements for low-bandwidth environments.
The proposed system offers clear advantages over existing manual and basic digital methods: it introduces predictive and prescriptive intelligence, automates insight generation, reduces administrative overhead, enhances retention through proactive personalization, and scales naturally with business growth. While limitations exist — such as dependency on initial data quality for recommendation accuracy and the absence of a native mobile application — the design remains highly appropriate for the target context of resource-constrained Nigerian SMEs.

3.9  High-Level Model of the Proposed System
The high-level model gives an overall picture of how the intelligent CRM system is structured and how its main parts connect.
The system is divided into four key layers that work together:
 Presentation Layer: The user-facing part. It includes the login page, dashboard, customer profile views, interaction forms, analytics charts, and recommendation display. This layer is built with HTML, CSS, Bootstrap, and JavaScript so it looks clean and works on any device.
 Application Layer: The core logic handled by Django. It processes user requests, manages authentication, handles business rules, coordinates data flow, and calls the intelligence layer when needed. Django views, models, and REST API endpoints form this layer.
  Data Layer: The storage part using PostgreSQL or SQLite. It holds all information: user accounts, customer profiles, purchase history, interaction logs, and generated recommendations. Data is stored in related tables for quick access and updates.
Intelligence Layer: The smart component. It includes the recommendation engine (using scikit-learn collaborative filtering) and basic analytics processing. This layer reads historical data, computes suggestions, and produces trend insights.
How the layers interact
A user logs in through the presentation layer → the application layer verifies and loads the dashboard → when the user adds a sale or views a customer, the application layer saves or retrieves data from the data layer → the intelligence layer analyzes the data and sends back recommendations or charts → the presentation layer shows everything to the user.
This model ensures separation of concerns: the frontend focuses on display, backend on logic, database on storage, and ML on smart features. It makes the system modular, easier to maintain, and scalable.

•  Logged out → login page.
•  Logged in → dashboard view.
•  Viewing customer → profile details and history.
•  Adding interaction → form submission and save.
•  Requesting recommendation → trigger ML and display results.
3.10.5 Algorithmic Design and Mathematical Modelling
The recommendation engine uses collaborative filtering with cosine similarity:
Similarity between two customers = (A · B) / (|A| |B|), where A and B are purchase vectors.
Top-k similar customers are found, and their items are recommended to the target customer.
Analytics uses basic statistics (sums, averages, counts) for charts.

•  Interactions (id, customer_id, type, note, date)
